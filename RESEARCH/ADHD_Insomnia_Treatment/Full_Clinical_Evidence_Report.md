# Clinical Evidence: Insomnia Treatment in ADHD Patients
## Focus on ADHD Medications + Sleep Aids Combinations

**Date:** November 22, 2025
**Research Scope:** Clinical studies, safety data, and guidelines for treating insomnia in ADHD patients, with specific emphasis on DORA medications and combination therapies

---

## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

### Key Findings

1. **Prevalence**: Insomnia affects 29% of stimulant-treated children with ADHD vs 10% of untreated children; 80% of adults with ADHD experience sleep issues
2. **DORA + ADHD Evidence Gap**: No published clinical trials or case reports specifically examining dual orexin receptor antagonists (DORAs) in ADHD patients
3. **Best Supported Treatment**: Melatonin (3-6 mg) combined with sleep hygiene shows strongest evidence in ADHD populations
4. **Safety**: No serious safety signals identified for stimulant + sleep aid combinations, though evidence remains limited
5. **Guideline Gap**: No FDA-approved sleep medications for pediatric ADHD; adult ADHD treatment guidelines pending (expected 2025)

---

## SECTION 1: PREVALENCE OF INSOMNIA IN ADHD

### Children with ADHD

**Evidence Quality: A** (Multiple prospective studies, systematic reviews)

- **Baseline prevalence**: 19.3% of children with ADHD experience moderate-to-severe sleep problems at least weekly vs 6.2% of healthy controls (Corkum et al., PMC3441938)
- **Stimulant-induced insomnia**: 29% of stimulant-treated children display nightly insomnia (>30 min sleep onset latency) vs 10% of untreated ADHD children (Corkum et al., PMC3441938)
- **Psychiatric comorbidity comparison**: 13.3% of psychiatric controls without ADHD show similar sleep problems

**Citation:**
Corkum P, Davidson F, Macpherson M. A framework for the assessment and treatment of sleep problems in children with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder. Pediatr Clin North Am. 2011;58(3):667-683. PMC3441938.

### Adults with ADHD

**Evidence Quality: B** (Cross-sectional studies, cohort studies)

- **Prevalence**: 80% of ADHD adults report sleep issues; 43% describe insomnia as "significant" vs <10% in general population
- **Specific complaints**: Later bedtimes, sleep onset difficulties exceeding one hour, daytime somnolence
- **Study population**: 182 adults with ADHD vs 117 controls (Surman et al., referenced in PMC3441938)

## SECTION 2: ADHD MEDICATION EFFECTS ON SLEEP

### Methylphenidate (Stimulant)

**Evidence Quality: A** (Randomized controlled trials with objective sleep measures)

**Primary Study: Sangal et al. - Crossover RCT**
- **Design**: Randomized, double-blind, crossover trial
- **Sample**: 85 children with ADHD
- **Duration**: Approximately 7 weeks per medication arm
- **Dosing**: Thrice-daily methylphenidate vs twice-daily atomoxetine

**Sleep Outcomes:**
- **Sleep onset latency increase**: 39.2 minutes (MPH) vs 12.1 minutes (atomoxetine), p < 0.001
- **Insomnia incidence**: Significantly greater with methylphenidate vs atomoxetine
- **Polysomnography**: Confirmed actigraphy findings

**Subjective Reports (Child Diaries):**
- Harder to get up in morning with MPH
- Took longer to fall asleep with MPH
- Worse sleep quality with MPH

**Full Citation:**
Sangal RB, Owens J, Allen AJ, Sutton V, Schuh K, Kelsey D. Effects of atomoxetine and methylphenidate on sleep in children with ADHD. Sleep. 2006;29(12):1573-1585. PMID: 17252888. https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article-pdf/29/12/1573/13663108/sleep-29-12-1573.pdf

**Dose-Dependent Effects:**
- Higher doses associated with greater insomnia risk
- 54 mg OROS methylphenidate: increased severe insomnia in younger/smaller patients (Stein et al., referenced in PMC3441938)

### Atomoxetine (Non-Stimulant)

**Evidence Quality: A** (Multiple RCTs with objective measures)

**Sleep Effects:**
- **Sleep onset latency**: Beneficial effect of approximately 12 minutes improvement (Sangal et al., 2006)
- **Trade-off**: More frequent night awakenings vs methylphenidate
- **Primary adverse event**: Somnolence (especially early treatment)

**Timing Considerations:**
- Evening administration minimizes daytime somnolence
- May reduce ADHD symptom control during waking hours

**Drug Interaction Safety - Atomoxetine + Trazodone:**

**Evidence Quality: B** (PBPK modeling study)

**Study:** Oggianu et al., 2020 - Physiologically-based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling

**Key Findings:**
- Trazodone AUC and Cmax predicted ratios: 1.06 and 1.05 in poor metabolizers; 1.01 and 1.01 in extensive metabolizers
- **Clinical significance**: Negligible interaction; no dose adjustment needed
- **Conclusion**: "Trazodone can be used concurrently with atomoxetine"

**Full Citation:**
Oggianu L, Bertera FM, Kenworthy KE, Cella M, Borsini F, de Wildt SN. Estimation of an Appropriate Dose of Trazodone for Pediatric Insomnia and the Potential for a Trazodone-Atomoxetine Interaction. CPT Pharmacometrics Syst Pharmacol. 2020;9(2):88-98. PMID: 31975576. PMC7020267. https://doi.org/10.1002/psp4.12480

### Alpha-2 Agonists (Clonidine, Guanfacine)

**Evidence Quality: B-C** (Limited RCTs, retrospective data, case series)

**Clonidine:**
- Used off-label for stimulant-induced insomnia
- **Somnolence rates**: 20-40% as monotherapy; 15-20% with stimulant co-administration (PMC3441938)
- **Dosing for sleep**: 0.05-0.1 mg at bedtime
- **Efficacy**: Significantly improved subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, and sleep disturbance (PMC9536883)
- **Population**: Particularly effective in females, 13-24 age group, and those with ADHD/mood disorders

**Full Citation - Clonidine Effectiveness:**
Ingrassia M, Turco M. The effectiveness of clonidine in child and adolescent sleep disorders - a systematic review. Psychiatr Pol. 2022;56(5):1121-1136. PMC9536883.

**Guanfacine:**
- **Extended-release formulation**: Approved as monotherapy or adjunct to stimulants for ADHD
- **Sleep effects**: FAILED to show improvement vs placebo in RCT (Anand et al., 2017)
- **Concerns**: Reduced REM and total sleep time in some studies
- **Safety**: Generally well-tolerated when combined with stimulants (77.3% experienced mild-moderate adverse events, low discontinuation) (PMC2861960)

**Full Citation - Guanfacine + Stimulant Safety:**
Hammerness P, Wilens T, Mick E, Spencer T, Doyle R, McCreary M, Becker J, Biederman J. Cardiovascular effects of longer-term, high-dose OROS methylphenidate and mixed amphetamine salts in adolescents with attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder. J Pediatr. 2009;155(1):84-89. PMC2861960.

## SECTION 3: SLEEP MEDICATIONS IN ADHD POPULATIONS

### 3.1 Melatonin

**Evidence Quality: A-B** (Multiple RCTs, systematic reviews, long-term follow-up)

#### Clinical Trial Data - ADHD-Specific

**Study 1: Van der Heijden et al., 2007**
- **Design**: Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial
- **Sample**: 105 medication-free children, ages 6-12, with ADHD and chronic sleep onset insomnia
- **Dosing**: 3 mg or 6 mg melatonin
- **Duration**: 4 weeks

**Sleep Outcomes:**
- Sleep onset advanced by **26.9 ± 47.8 minutes** with melatonin
- Sleep onset delayed by 10.5 ± 37.4 minutes with placebo
- **Statistical significance**: p < 0.0001
- Enhanced total time asleep
- Advanced circadian rhythms of sleep-wake and endogenous melatonin

**Behavioral/Cognitive Outcomes:**
- **No effect** on problem behavior
- **No effect** on cognitive performance
- **No effect** on quality of life

**Full Citation:**
Van der Heijden KB, Smits MG, Van Someren EJ, Ridderinkhof KR, Gunning WB. Effect of melatonin on sleep, behavior, and cognition in ADHD and chronic sleep-onset insomnia. J Am Acad Child Adolesc Psychiatry. 2007;46(2):233-241. PMID: 17242627.

**Study 2: Weiss et al., 2006 - Melatonin + Stimulants + Sleep Hygiene**
- **Design**: Double-blind, placebo-controlled, crossover trial
- **Sample**: 27 stimulant-treated children (ages 6-14) with ADHD and initial insomnia
- **Intervention**: Sleep hygiene first; non-responders randomized to melatonin 5 mg vs placebo
- **Duration**: 30 days

**Key Findings:**
- Combined sleep hygiene + melatonin = safe and effective
- ADHD stimulant benefits maintained
- Improved sleep outcomes

**Full Citation:**
Weiss MD, Wasdell MB, Bomben MM, Rea KJ, Freeman RD. Sleep hygiene and melatonin treatment for children and adolescents with ADHD and initial insomnia. J Am Acad Child Adolesc Psychiatry. 2006;45(5):512-519. PMID: 16670647.

#### Long-term Effectiveness

**Study: Hoebert et al., 2009**
- **Design**: Long-term follow-up of children with ADHD and chronic sleep onset insomnia
- **Effectiveness rate**: 88% judged melatonin effective long-term
- **Safety**: No serious adverse events with chronic use

**Full Citation:**
Hoebert M, van der Heijden KB, van Geijlswijk IM, Smits MG. Long-term follow-up of melatonin treatment in children with ADHD and chronic sleep onset insomnia. J Pineal Res. 2009;47(1):1-7. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-079X.2009.00681.x

#### Systematic Review - ASD/ADHD

**Study: Jain et al., 2020**
- **Scope**: RCTs on melatonin in pediatric ASD and neurodevelopmental disorders
- **Findings**: Statistically significant improvement in sleep duration and sleep latency vs placebo
- **Limitation**: Only ONE RCT specifically in ADHD population (Mohammadi et al., 2012)

**Mohammadi et al., 2012 - ADHD/ASD Study:**
- **Sample**: 50 children (7-12 years) with ASD and ADHD
- **Groups**: 26 melatonin (3-6 mg/day) vs 24 methylphenidate-only
- **Duration**: 8 weeks
- **Results**: Melatonin did NOT show statistically significant improvement in total sleep duration (p=0.19)
- **Conclusion**: Negative finding in ADHD-specific population

**Full Citation:**
Jain SV, Horn PS, Simakajornboon N, Beebe DW, Holland K, Byars AW, Glauser TA. Melatonin improves sleep in children with epilepsy: a randomized, double-blind, crossover study. Sleep Med. 2015;16(5):637-644. Systematic review: PMC7325410.

#### Safety Profile

**Evidence Quality: A** (Long-term studies up to 2 years)

**Adverse Events (52-week study, Maras et al.):**
- Fatigue: 18.9%
- Vomiting: 16.8%
- Mood swings: 13.7%
- Upper respiratory infection: 10.5%

**Meta-analysis finding:**
- Headache and mood changes: 3.44 times more frequent vs placebo

**Dosing:**
- **Effective range**: 2-10 mg/day
- **Higher doses** (>10 mg): NOT more efficacious
- **Quality concern**: Dietary supplements lack FDA oversight; actual content varies

**Guideline Recommendations:**
- **International Pediatric Sleep Association**: Endorses 2-10 mg for developmentally disabled children age 2+
- **American Academy of Sleep Medicine**: Supports "weak evidence" for timing-specific melatonin in delayed sleep-wake phase disorder

### 3.3 L-Theanine

**Evidence Quality: C** (Single RCT)

**Study:** Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in boys with ADHD

**Findings:**
- Improvements in sleep-onset latency and total sleep duration (Anand et al., 2017 systematic review)
- **Quality**: Well-tolerated with mild-moderate adverse events

### 3.4 Benzodiazepines and Z-Drugs (Zolpidem, Eszopiclone)

**Evidence Quality: C-D** (Single pediatric RCTs, no efficacy shown)

#### Zolpidem

**Study:** Controlled clinical trial in children 6-17 years with ADHD-associated insomnia

**Findings:**
- **Failed** to show improvement vs placebo (Anand et al., 2017)
- **Safety concern**: Associated with neuropsychiatric adverse effects
- **Not recommended** for pediatric ADHD

**Full Citation:**
Anand S, Tong H, Besag FM, Chan EW, Cortese S, Wong IC. Safety, Tolerability and Efficacy of Drugs for Treating Behavioural Insomnia in Children with Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder: A Systematic Review with Methodological Quality Assessment. Paediatr Drugs. 2017;19(3):235-250. PMID: 28391425. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40272-017-0224-6

#### Eszopiclone

**Study:** Single RCT in pediatric ADHD (highest quality rating in Anand et al. systematic review)

**Findings:**
- **Failed** to show improvement vs placebo
- Well-tolerated (mild-moderate adverse events)
- Favorable profile BUT no efficacy

#### General Benzodiazepine Concerns

**Evidence Quality: B** (Safety surveillance, discontinuation studies)

**Safety Issues:**
- Risk of dependence and tolerance
- Cognitive impairment
- Increased suicide risk within 2 weeks of treatment cessation
- Oral overdose rarely toxic unless combined with other agents

**Concomitant Use Risks:**
- In 2020: Nearly 29% of benzodiazepine toxic incidents involved multiple substances (opioid, stimulant, or alcohol)
- Serious adverse effects typically occur with co-ingestion

### 3.5 Trazodone

**Evidence Quality: D** (No RCTs in pediatric ADHD; off-label use based on clinical practice)

#### Prescribing Patterns

**Study: Legler et al., 2022 - Oregon Medicaid Data 2012-2016**
- **Prevalence**: 8.4% (2,705 of 32,134) children with ADHD prescribed trazodone
- **Gender difference**: 10.1% females vs 7.7% males
- **Age pattern**: Peak usage around age 15; most prescriptions in children 10+ years

**Full Citation:**
Legler M, Springer P, Hale A, Bowen E, Carlson K, Chung M, DeShaw P, Haxby DG. Trazodone Prescribing for Children With Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder on Medicaid in Oregon. J Child Adolesc Psychopharmacol. 2022;32(1):54-60. PMC8837222. https://doi.org/10.1089/cap.2021.0072

#### Safety and Efficacy

**Evidence Base:**
- **Never FDA-approved** for insomnia in children or adults
- No RCTs in pediatric ADHD population
- Adult insomnia trials: "limited in number, short term, and of variable quality"
- Tolerability and long-term safety: NOT demonstrated

**Clinical Concerns:**
- Off-label use without national guidelines or clinical evidence
- Used for "sleep-inducing effects" despite lack of pediatric data
- **Contraindication concern**: May be prescribed for bipolar-related sleep issues despite being "not advised for sleep disorders in this population"

**Trazodone + SSRI Combination Risk:**
- Combined with SSRIs: **3 times more likely** to experience self-harm ideation
- Mechanism: mCPP (active metabolite) accumulation
- Reduced antidepressant effectiveness

#### Drug Interaction with Atomoxetine

**See Section 2 (Atomoxetine) above**

**Summary:** No significant pharmacokinetic interaction; safe to combine

---

### 3.6 Mirtazapine

**Evidence Quality: D** (Open-label study in autism; survey data)

**Available Evidence:**
- One open-label study in children with autism and pervasive developmental disorders
- **Response rate**: 34.6% showed improvements in aggression, anxiety, and insomnia
- **Perceived effectiveness** (Canadian survey of child psychiatrists treating depression): 55.4%

**Limitation:** No RCTs in pediatric ADHD; extrapolation from autism and depression populations

---

### 3.7 Gabapentin

**Evidence Quality: D** (Single case series)

**Study:** Case series of 23 children

**Findings:**
- **Benefit rate**: 78% of subjects
- **Adverse event**: Agitated awakenings occurred

**Limitation:** No controlled trials

## SECTION 4: DUAL OREXIN RECEPTOR ANTAGONISTS (DORAs)

### 4.1 Overview of DORAs

**FDA-Approved DORAs for Insomnia:**
1. **Suvorexant (Belsomra)** - Approved August 2014
2. **Lemborexant (Dayvigo)** - Approved December 2019
3. **Daridorexant (Quviviq)** - Approved January 2022

**Mechanism of Action:**
- Block orexin receptor 1 and orexin receptor 2
- Suppress wakefulness by blocking orexin neuropeptides
- Promote both REM and non-REM sleep
- Do NOT suppress overall brain activity (unlike benzodiazepines/Z-drugs acting on GABA receptors)

**General Efficacy (Adult Insomnia Populations):**
- Reduce sleep onset latency
- Increase total sleep time
- Improve sleep continuity and efficiency
- Network meta-analysis: Favorable efficacy profile

**Full Citation:**
Janto K, Prichard JR, Pusalavidyasagar S. An update on dual orexin receptor antagonists and their potential role in insomnia therapeutics. J Clin Sleep Med. 2018;14(8):1399-1408. https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.7282

---

### 4.2 DORA Evidence in ADHD: CRITICAL GAP

**Evidence Quality: E** (Evidence gap - no published studies)

**Comprehensive Literature Search Results:**
- **NO published clinical trials** of DORAs specifically in ADHD patients
- **NO case reports** of DORA + ADHD medication combinations
- **NO case series** in ADHD populations
- **NO pharmacovigilance data** publicly available on concomitant use with stimulants

**Related Neurobiology:**
- Orexin system dysregulation implicated in ADHD pathophysiology
- Study finding: Decreased serum orexin A levels in drug-naive children with ADHD (p=0.001) (Gunes et al., 2019)
- Orexin associated with attention symptoms in ADHD
- "Orexin might be a neurobiological etiological factor in ADHD"

**Full Citation:**
Gunes S, Ekinci O, Feyzioglu A, Ekinci N, Karadag M, Yesilyurt O. Decreased serum orexin A levels in drug-naive children with attention deficit and hyperactivity disorder. Neurol Sci. 2019;40(3):593-598. PMID: 30617449. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10072-018-3692-8

**Preclinical Evidence - Methamphetamine Model:**
- Almorexant (DORA, not FDA-approved) blocked sleep-disrupting effects of methamphetamine in rhesus monkeys
- Orexin antagonists (suvorexant, MK-1064) dose-dependently improved actigraphy-based sleep in methamphetamine-treated monkeys
- Relevance: Suggests potential for DORAs to address stimulant-induced sleep disruption

**Full Citation:**
Stocker H, Rizzo D, Hintermann S, Severin A, Derouet F, Fischli W, Boss C, Jenck F. The dual orexin receptor antagonist almorexant blocks the sleep-disrupting and daytime stimulant effects of methamphetamine in rhesus monkeys. Addict Biol. 2022;27(1):e13099. https://doi.org/10.1111/adb.13099

---

### 4.3 DORAs in Psychiatric Comorbidity

**Evidence Quality: B** (Systematic review, multiple RCTs in psychiatric populations)

**Study: Yuan et al., 2024 - Systematic Review**

**Psychiatric Disorders Studied:**
- Depression
- Anxiety disorders
- Post-traumatic stress disorder
- Mild cognitive impairment
- **NOTE: ADHD NOT included** in reviewed studies

**Key Safety Findings:**
- DORAs significantly improved sleep WITHOUT worsening comorbid psychiatric disorders
- No new adverse events in psychiatric populations vs traditional insomnia trials
- "No deterioration of comorbid depression was observed with DORA treatment"

**Depression-Specific Data:**
- Studies included up to 49.1% of patients with baseline depression
- Only one case of worsening depression observed as adverse effect (in 15.6% depression cohort)
- **Caution**: Previous studies found suvorexant associated with seemingly dose-dependent worsening of depression and suicidal ideation; daridorexant more likely to cause depression

**Common Adverse Events:**
- Somnolence, abnormal dreams, fatigue, dry mouth (higher than placebo)
- "General disorders and administration site conditions," "psychiatric disorders," and "nervous system disorders" were top three in frequency
- Predominantly mild adverse events (4.1-14.8% overall incidence)

**Discontinuation Rates:**
- Suvorexant: 45.0% (Add-on group), 24.4% (Switch group)
- Lemborexant: 22.6%

**Full Citation:**
Yuan Y, Wu W, Chen M, Guo XY, Yang N, Li CY, Sun Y, Ni SY, Zhang L, Zhao Y, Shi L, Sun L. Orexin receptor antagonists in the treatment of insomnia associated with psychiatric disorders: a systematic review. Transl Psychiatry. 2024;14(1):357. PMC11401906. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41398-024-03087-4

---

### 4.4 DORA Pharmacokinetics and Drug Interactions

**Evidence Quality: A** (Comprehensive pharmacokinetic studies)

**Metabolism:**
- All three DORAs metabolized by **CYP3A** (predominantly CYP3A4)
- Guidance exists for combining with CYP3A inhibitors or inducers

**Pharmacokinetic Profiles:**

| Parameter | Suvorexant | Lemborexant | Daridorexant |
|-----------|------------|-------------|--------------|
| Clearance | 4.81 L/h | 22.7 L/h | 2.4 L/h (at 500 ng/ml) |
| Elimination half-life | ~12 hours | ~18-55 hours | ~8 hours |
| Next-day effects | Potential carryover | Longer duration | Minimal carryover |

**Clinical Implications:**
- Daridorexant: Shorter half-life = "exposure primarily when in bed" with "no carry over effects the next day and no accumulation"
- Suvorexant/Lemborexant: Longer half-lives may affect morning alertness

**Drug Interaction Recommendations:**
- **CYP3A4 strong inhibitors**: May need to DECREASE DORA dose
- **CYP3A4 strong inducers**: May need to INCREASE DORA dose
- Full interaction details require consultation of prescribing information

**Full Citations:**
Atack JR. Comparative pharmacology of the 3 marketed dual orexin antagonists - daridorexant, lemborexant, and suvorexant - part 1: pharmacokinetic profiles. J Clin Pharmacol. 2023;63(1):7-18. PMID: 36355586.

Atack JR. Comparative pharmacology of the 3 marketed dual orexin antagonists - daridorexant, lemborexant, and suvorexant - part 2: principal drug metabolizing enzyme, drug-drug interactions, and effects of liver and renal impairment on metabolism. J Clin Pharmacol. 2023;63(3):279-292. PMID: 36649550.

**STIMULANT INTERACTION DATA:**
- **NOT STUDIED** in published literature
- No specific guidance on ADHD medication combinations
- Theoretical concern: Both ADHD stimulants and DORAs affect alertness/arousal systems via different mechanisms

### 4.5 Comparative Effectiveness: DORAs vs Other Sleep Medications

**Evidence Quality: B** (Network meta-analyses, systematic reviews - general insomnia populations)

**General Insomnia Populations (NOT ADHD-specific):**

**Efficacy Rankings:**
- Eszopiclone and lemborexant: Favorable efficacy profiles
- Suvorexant, daridorexant, benzodiazepines, trazodone: Effective in acute treatment

**Safety/Tolerability Rankings:**
1. **DORAs**: Most favorable adverse effect profile; low dependence risk; no cognitive impairment concerns
2. **Melatonin**: Generally safe; quality control issues
3. **Z-drugs/Benzodiazepines**: Risk of dependence, tolerance, cognitive issues, amnesia, next-day grogginess

**Advantages of DORAs over GABAergic Agents:**
- Promote sleep without suppressing overall brain activity
- No amnesia risk
- No next-day grogginess (especially daridorexant)
- Patients report feeling "more alert and able to perform daily tasks"
- Low dependence/abuse potential
- Expected low dependence due to "differential mechanism of action from older benzodiazepine hypnotics"

**Full Citations:**
"Sleeping Pills: Benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, DORAs." Benzodiazepine Information Coalition. https://www.benzoinfo.com/sleeping-pills/

Rosenberg R, Murphy P, Zammit G, Mayleben D, Kumar D, Dhadda S, Filippov G, LoPresti A, Moline M. Comparison of lemborexant with placebo and zolpidem tartrate extended release for the treatment of older adults with insomnia disorder: a phase 3 randomized clinical trial. JAMA Netw Open. 2019;2(12):e1918254.

**ADHD-Specific Comparative Data:**
- **DOES NOT EXIST** for DORAs
- Melatonin remains best-studied in ADHD populations

## SECTION 5: SAFETY OF COMBINATION THERAPY

### 5.1 Stimulants + Sedative-Hypnotics: General Considerations

**Evidence Quality: C** (Regulatory review documents, clinical practice patterns)

**Utah Medicaid Drug Utilization Review (2024):**

**Clinical Appropriateness Factors:**
1. **Timing consideration**: Stimulants and sedative-hypnotics administered at distinct times during day
2. **Guideline-concordant approach**: Sedative-hypnotic reasonable for patients who failed:
   - Behavioral interventions
   - Stimulant adjustments (dose, timing, formulation)
3. **Concomitant use patterns**: Stimulant users may take sedatives to "calm excessive jitteriness"

**Safety Concerns:**
- Emergency department visits related to benzodiazepine overdose usually involve concomitant ingestions
- 2020: Nearly 29% of benzodiazepine toxic incidents involved multiple substances (opioid, stimulant, alcohol)
- Oral benzodiazepine overdose alone rarely causes serious toxicity

**Clinical Recommendation:**
- Concomitant use can be appropriate when guideline-recommended treatments fail
- Requires careful patient selection and monitoring
- Administration timing should be optimized

**Full Citation:**
Utah Department of Health and Human Services, Division of Medicaid and Health Financing. "Concomitant Use of Stimulants for ADHD and Sedative-Hypnotics." Drug Utilization Review, July 2024. https://medicaid-documents.dhhs.utah.gov/pharmacy/drugutilization/files/Criteria Review Documents/2024/2024.07 Stimulants and Sedative-Hypnotics Concomitant Use.pdf

---

### 5.2 Specific Combination Safety Data

#### Stimulants + Melatonin

**Evidence Quality: A** (Multiple RCTs)

**Safety Profile:**
- Well-established safety in combination with stimulants
- RCT (Weiss et al., 2006): Safe and effective combination
- Maintains ADHD symptom control while improving sleep
- Long-term use (up to 2 years): No serious adverse events

**Recommendation:** FIRST-LINE adjunctive therapy for stimulant-induced insomnia

---

#### Stimulants + Alpha-2 Agonists (Clonidine/Guanfacine)

**Evidence Quality: A-B** (RCTs for guanfacine; retrospective/observational for clonidine)

**Guanfacine Extended-Release + Stimulants:**
- FDA-approved combination
- Safety study: 77.3% experienced adverse events related to guanfacine (mostly mild-moderate)
- Low discontinuation rates
- Cardiovascular monitoring recommended

**Clonidine + Stimulants:**
- Common off-label practice
- Somnolence rates: 15-20% when combined with stimulants
- **Cautions**: Orthostatic hypotension, rebound hypertension with sudden discontinuation

**Recommendation:** Generally safe combinations with appropriate monitoring

---

#### Atomoxetine + Trazodone

**Evidence Quality: B** (PBPK modeling)

**Safety Profile:**
- No significant pharmacokinetic interaction
- Trazodone AUC/Cmax ratios approach unity (1.01-1.06)
- No dose adjustment needed
- Safe to combine

**Full Citation:** See Section 2 (Atomoxetine)

---

#### Stimulants + DORAs

**Evidence Quality: E** (No published data)

**Current Status:**
- **NO published safety data** on this combination
- **NO clinical trials** examining concurrent use
- **NO case reports** in medical literature
- **NO FDA adverse event signals** publicly reported

**Theoretical Considerations:**
1. **Different mechanisms**: Stimulants enhance wakefulness via monoamines; DORAs block wakefulness via orexin
2. **Timing separation**: Could mitigate interaction risk
3. **Preclinical data**: DORAs reversed methamphetamine-induced sleep disruption in monkeys (suggests compatibility)

**Recommendation:**
- Data insufficient for evidence-based recommendation
- Would require careful clinical monitoring if attempted
- Consider alternative sleep medications with established ADHD safety data first (melatonin, clonidine)

---

#### Stimulants + Benzodiazepines/Z-drugs

**Evidence Quality: C-D** (Limited pediatric data; safety concerns from adult/toxicology data)

**Pediatric ADHD:**
- Zolpidem, eszopiclone: Failed to show efficacy in RCTs
- No established safety profile for chronic combined use
- NOT recommended based on lack of efficacy

**Safety Concerns:**
- Dependence and tolerance risk
- Cognitive impairment
- Higher overdose risk with polypharmacy
- Paradoxical agitation in some pediatric patients

**Recommendation:** AVOID in pediatric ADHD due to lack of efficacy and safety concerns

## SECTION 6: CLINICAL PRACTICE GUIDELINES

### 6.1 American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM)

**Pediatric Insomnia Consensus Statement (2005)**

**Evidence Quality:** Consensus guideline based on limited evidence

**Key Recommendations:**
1. **Medication NOT first-line**: Should be diagnostically driven and implemented in conjunction with behavioral treatment and sleep hygiene
2. **No FDA-approved medications** for pediatric insomnia exist
3. **Target populations**: Children with neurodevelopmental disorders may benefit from pharmacotherapy
4. **Comprehensive approach**: View medication within context of broader treatment plan

**Full Citations:**
Mindell JA, Emslie G, Blumer J, et al. Pharmacologic management of insomnia in children and adolescents: consensus statement. Pediatrics. 2006;117(6):e1223-e1232. PMID: 16740821.

Owens JA, Babcock D, Blumer J, et al. The use of pharmacotherapy in the treatment of pediatric insomnia in primary care: rational approaches. A consensus meeting summary. J Clin Sleep Med. 2005;1(1):49-59. PMID: 17561616.

---

**AASM Adult Insomnia Guidelines (2017)**

**Pharmacologic Treatment Guideline:**
- Focus on chronic insomnia in adults
- Does NOT specifically address ADHD populations
- Most recent comprehensive pharmacologic guideline

### 6.5 Guideline Summary: ADHD Insomnia Management

**Evidence-Based Treatment Hierarchy:**

**Tier 1 (Strongest Evidence):**
1. Sleep hygiene and behavioral interventions (ALL guidelines)
2. ADHD medication optimization (timing, dose, formulation)
3. Melatonin 3-6 mg if behavioral interventions fail (RCT support)

**Tier 2 (Moderate Evidence):**
4. Clonidine for stimulant-induced insomnia (retrospective data, case series)
5. CBT-I in adolescents/adults (limited pediatric data)

**Tier 3 (Weak/Off-label, Limited Evidence):**
6. Trazodone (widespread use but no RCT support)
7. Alpha-2 agonists as ADHD treatment with sleep benefit (guanfacine failed to show sleep benefit)

**NOT Recommended (Negative/No Efficacy Data):**
- Zolpidem (neuropsychiatric adverse effects; no efficacy in ADHD)
- Eszopiclone (no efficacy vs placebo in pediatric ADHD)
- Benzodiazepines (dependence risk; no pediatric ADHD efficacy data)

**Unknown/Insufficient Data:**
- DORAs (no ADHD-specific studies)
- Mirtazapine (limited autism data only)
- Gabapentin (single small case series)

**Rating Scale (A-E):**
- **A**: High-quality RCTs, systematic reviews, meta-analyses with consistent findings
- **B**: RCTs with some limitations, well-designed cohort studies, consistent observational data
- **C**: Case-control studies, retrospective analyses, inconsistent RCT results
- **D**: Case series, expert opinion, extrapolated from other populations
- **E**: Evidence gap - no published studies or only theoretical rationale

## SECTION 8: CRITICAL KNOWLEDGE GAPS

### 8.1 DORA + ADHD Research Gaps

**Missing Evidence:**
1. **NO clinical trials** of any DORA in ADHD populations
2. **NO safety data** on DORA + stimulant combinations
3. **NO safety data** on DORA + atomoxetine combinations
4. **NO pharmacovigilance reports** published on concomitant use
5. **NO case reports** documenting clinical experience

**Why This Gap Matters:**
- DORAs represent novel mechanism with theoretical advantages over GABAergic agents
- Orexin system dysregulation implicated in ADHD pathophysiology
- Preclinical data suggest DORAs may counteract stimulant-induced sleep disruption
- 29% of stimulant-treated ADHD children experience insomnia
- Current off-label options (trazodone) lack RCT support

**Research Needed:**
1. **Phase 2 RCT**: DORA vs placebo in stimulant-treated ADHD patients with insomnia
2. **Pharmacokinetic study**: Interaction potential between stimulants/atomoxetine and DORAs
3. **Retrospective cohort**: Real-world safety/effectiveness data from patients prescribed both
4. **Pharmacovigilance analysis**: FAERS database mining for adverse event signals

---

### 8.2 Other Research Gaps

1. **Long-term safety** of sleep medication use in pediatric ADHD (>2 years)
2. **Comparative effectiveness trials** of different sleep medications in ADHD
3. **Optimal dosing** of melatonin in ADHD (current range 2-10 mg is broad)
4. **CBT-I validation** in pediatric ADHD populations
5. **Genetic/biomarker predictors** of sleep medication response in ADHD
6. **Combination therapy optimization**: Best sequencing and timing
7. **Adult ADHD insomnia**: Most studies in pediatric populations

## SECTION 9: CLINICAL RECOMMENDATIONS

### 9.1 Stepwise Approach to ADHD-Related Insomnia

**Based on Current Evidence:**

**Step 1: Optimize ADHD Medication**
- Assess timing of stimulant administration (earliest possible morning dose)
- Consider shorter-acting stimulant formulation
- Evaluate dose (highest risk in younger/smaller patients on higher doses)
- Consider switch to atomoxetine if sleep disruption severe and ADHD control adequate

**Evidence:** Expert consensus, observational data (Grade C recommendation)

---

**Step 2: Sleep Hygiene and Behavioral Interventions**
- Consistent bedtime routines
- Sleep environment optimization
- Limit screen time before bed
- Regular sleep-wake schedule
- CBT-I for adolescents/adults

**Evidence:** All clinical guidelines recommend (Grade A for general insomnia; Grade B for ADHD-specific)

---

**Step 3: Melatonin Trial**
- **Dosing**: Start 3 mg; may increase to 6 mg if needed
- **Timing**: 30-60 minutes before desired bedtime
- **Duration**: 4-8 week trial
- **Monitor**: Sleep onset latency, total sleep time, daytime functioning

**Evidence:** Multiple RCTs in ADHD (Grade A-B recommendation)

**Expected Outcomes:**
- Sleep onset advancement: ~27 minutes
- Maintains ADHD medication efficacy
- Safe for long-term use (up to 2 years studied)

---

**Step 4: Alpha-2 Agonist Consideration**
- **Clonidine**: 0.05-0.1 mg at bedtime for stimulant-induced insomnia
- **Guanfacine**: May help ADHD symptoms but does NOT improve sleep (avoid specifically for sleep)

**Evidence:** Clonidine - retrospective/case series (Grade B-C); Guanfacine - negative RCT for sleep (Grade C - recommend AGAINST for sleep indication)

**Monitoring:**
- Blood pressure (orthostatic hypotension risk)
- Heart rate
- Somnolence
- Taper slowly if discontinuing (rebound hypertension risk)

---

**Step 5: Consider Off-Label Options (Insufficient Evidence)**

**If Steps 1-4 fail:**
- **Trazodone**: Widely used but no RCT support in pediatric ADHD
  - Dosing: Pediatric insomnia dosing not established
  - Cautions: Contraindicated in bipolar; interaction risk with SSRIs
  - Safe to combine with atomoxetine (no PK interaction)

**Evidence:** Off-label practice patterns (Grade D recommendation - weak)

**Alternative:**
- **L-Theanine**: Single RCT showed benefit
- **Mirtazapine**: Limited autism data; 55% perceived effectiveness in depression-related sleep issues

---

**Step 6: Specialist Referral**

**Consider sleep medicine or child psychiatry consultation for:**
- Refractory insomnia despite Steps 1-5
- Suspected primary sleep disorder (sleep apnea, restless legs syndrome, etc.)
- Complex psychiatric comorbidity
- Safety concerns with polypharmacy

---

### 9.2 DORA Use in ADHD: Current Recommendations

**Clinical Question:** Should DORAs be used in ADHD patients with insomnia?

**Answer:** Insufficient evidence for recommendation

**Rationale:**
- No published clinical trials in ADHD populations
- No safety data on combinations with ADHD medications
- Alternative therapies with established ADHD safety profiles available (melatonin, clonidine)

**IF clinician considers DORA despite lack of data:**

**Prerequisites:**
1. Failed behavioral interventions
2. Failed ADHD medication optimization
3. Failed melatonin trial
4. Failed or contraindicated alpha-2 agonist
5. Informed consent documenting lack of ADHD-specific data
6. Close monitoring for:
   - Sleep efficacy (sleep diary, actigraphy)
   - ADHD symptom control
   - Mood changes (depression/suicidal ideation risk with suvorexant)
   - Next-day alertness
   - Any unusual adverse effects

**DORA Selection (if proceeding):**
- **Daridorexant**: Shortest half-life (~8 hours); minimal next-day effects; may be preferable for patients needing morning alertness
- **Lemborexant/Suvorexant**: Longer half-lives; potential next-day sedation concerns

**Drug Interaction Considerations:**
- All DORAs metabolized by CYP3A4
- Most ADHD medications do NOT significantly affect CYP3A4
- Atomoxetine: Minimal CYP3A4 inhibition (PBPK modeling showed negligible effect on trazodone)
- **Conclusion**: Low theoretical interaction risk, but clinical data lacking

---

### 9.3 Special Populations

**Preschool Children (Age <6):**
- Very limited pharmacotherapy data
- Behavioral interventions emphasized
- Melatonin: Some safety data available; consider if behavioral interventions fail

**Adolescents:**
- All medication options available
- CBT-I more feasible than in younger children
- Higher rates of off-label trazodone use observed
- Suicide risk monitoring essential with any psychiatric medication

**Adults with ADHD:**
- Most insomnia medication trials conducted in adults
- DORA data available in general adult insomnia (not ADHD-specific)
- Stimulant-induced insomnia remains common
- Pending adult ADHD treatment guidelines (2025)

**Comorbid Psychiatric Conditions:**
- **Depression**: DORAs appear safe (systematic review); monitor for worsening (especially suvorexant)
- **Anxiety**: DORAs appear safe in anxiety populations
- **Bipolar disorder**: Trazodone contraindicated; limited data on other options
- **Autism + ADHD**: Melatonin best-studied; high response rates

## SECTION 10: FUTURE RESEARCH PRIORITIES

### High-Priority Studies

1. **DORA RCT in Stimulant-Treated ADHD**
   - Design: Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled
   - Population: Children/adolescents ages 6-17 with ADHD on stable stimulant, experiencing insomnia
   - Intervention: Daridorexant (or other DORA) vs placebo
   - Duration: 12 weeks acute + 40-week extension
   - Outcomes: Sleep (PSG, actigraphy), ADHD symptoms, safety, quality of life
   - Sample size: 200+ to detect moderate effect sizes

2. **Comparative Effectiveness Trial**
   - Design: Head-to-head comparison
   - Interventions: Melatonin vs Clonidine vs DORA vs Trazodone
   - Population: ADHD with stimulant-induced insomnia
   - Pragmatic trial design in real-world settings

3. **Pharmacokinetic Study**
   - Evaluate drug-drug interactions:
     - Methylphenidate + each DORA
     - Amphetamine + each DORA
     - Atomoxetine + each DORA
   - Include cytochrome P450 genotyping

4. **Long-Term Safety Study**
   - Duration: 2+ years
   - Population: Pediatric and adult ADHD
   - Assess: Growth, development, cognitive function, mood, sleep architecture changes

5. **Biomarker Study**
   - Measure serum orexin levels in ADHD with/without insomnia
   - Predict DORA response based on orexin neurobiology
   - Genetic markers of sleep medication response
